To introduce the concept of marginal cost, let's use as an example a simple business: a hot dog cart owned by this guy, the Hot Dog Baron. The inputs for this business are the hot dog cart, which keeps the dogs warm and transports them; the ingredients for the hot dogs (the meat, the buns, the condiments); and labor, which is required to make and sell the hot dogs. Let's say that the Hot Dog Baron has three basic costs associated with operating the hot dog cart. The rental cost of the cart and the fee that the owner needs to pay to the city in order to get the license to operate on the street are fixed, and the owner needs to pay this amount regardless of the total amount of hot dogs that he produces and sells. In a later video, we'll refer to these as fixed costs. We also have the cost of ingredients for the hot dogs themselves; let's say each hot dog costs the owner $1, all in: bun, meat, ingredients. And finally, we have the cost of labor, the worker who actually assembles and sells the hot dogs, and let's say that labor costs $10 a day.

Okay, let's say that the Hot Dog Baron hires one worker, and in one day, that one worker can sell 10 hot dogs. Okay, if the Hot Dog Baron decides that he wants to produce and sell more hot dogs, it's not going to be possible to rent another cart tomorrow; they're rented on a monthly basis. So the only thing that he can do is hire more labor. But these new workers have to take the hot dogs from the cart and walk a little bit further away to try and sell the hot dogs. As a result, they're less efficient than the first guy, who gets to stand right by the cart and just hand the hot dogs out. So if the Hot Dog Baron wants to increase his production and sales of hot dogs to 20, he needs to hire two more workers, for a total of three workers. So let's say if he wants to sell 30 hot dogs, he needs to hire, in total, six workers. Okay, so this is the setup. Let's use it to better understand how increasing the amount of hot dogs that are being produced and sold affects the costs of the business. In order to do that, we need to introduce the idea of marginal cost. Marginal cost is defined as the additional cost of producing and selling one more unit of a good or service. It's calculated as the change in total cost divided by the change in total output. In the case of the hot dog vendor, marginal cost would be: how much more money does the hot dog vendor have to pay to produce one more hot dog?

So if the Hot Dog Baron decides to shut down for the day and produce and sell zero hot dogs, he still needs to pay the cost of rental for the cart, as well as the licensing fee to the city. These are fixed costs that don't change with the level of production. These costs don't change when the vendor decides to produce more or less, so they're actually not related to marginal cost, which is only considering the additional costs incurred when deciding to produce more.

Okay, so in order to produce and sell 10 hot dogs, the hot dog vendor has to buy the ingredients for 10 hot dogs, and he has to hire one worker. That worker costs $10, and we know that the ingredients cost $1 per dog, for a total of $10 for ingredients. So those first 10 hot dogs that are produced increase cost by a total of $20. Using our equation for the calculation of marginal cost: if we increase output from zero hot dogs to 10 hot dogs, we produced 10 more hot dogs, and our costs increased by a total of $20. $20 divided by 10 hot dogs is $2 per dog. The marginal cost of the first hot dog is $2, as well as the second, the third, the fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh, eighth, ninth, tenth hot dog. The Baron needs to pay an additional $2 to produce each one of those first 10 hot dogs.

Okay, so if the Hot Dog Baron decides that he wants to produce and sell more hot dogs, he needs to hire more labor. In order to increase output from 10 hot dogs to 20 hot dogs, an increase in total product, or total quantity, of 10, the Hot Dog Baron needs to hire two more workers, for a cost of $20, and pay for $10 more worth of raw ingredients, for a total increase in cost of $30. The marginal cost of the 11th hot dog, 12th hot dog, 13, 14, 15... 20th hot dog, each, is $3. Each one of those hot dogs cost the vendor an additional $3 to make. To increase production from 20 hot dogs to 30 hot dogs, the vendor now needs to hire three more workers. This increases total cost by $30 for the workers, plus $10 for the ingredients, for a total of $40. The marginal cost of the 21st, 22nd, 23rd... 30th hot dog is $4 each. And finally, to increase production to 40 hot dogs, he needs to hire four more workers and pay for 10 more hot dogs' worth of ingredients, which increases total cost by $50. So the marginal cost of these next 10 hot dogs is $5.

We can plot the marginal cost curve on a diagram. We put marginal cost on the vertical axis and quantity of hot dogs on the horizontal axis. The first 10 hot dogs increase cost by $2 each, so the marginal cost of each of those first 10 hot dogs is $2.
