In order to talk about allocative efficiency, we need to do a super quick review of marginal cost and marginal benefit. We remember that marginal benefit is the additional benefit that one gains from consuming one more unit of a good, like Twinkies. As more of a good is consumed, marginal benefit decreases. In fact, the demand curve is a marginal benefit curve. What that means is that consumers are only willing to pay money to buy one more unit of a good if the additional benefit that they get from consuming that good is greater than the price they need to pay to buy it.

We also understand from a previous video that marginal cost is the additional cost to businesses of producing and selling one more unit of a good. We've attempted to explain in a number of previous videos why marginal cost is expected to increase as more of a good is produced. It's a strange phenomenon; you don't have to like it, and we'll continue to explore the idea of increasing marginal cost when we get to production theory. But for now, the more that producers make of a good, the greater the additional cost of producing each additional unit becomes. Supply is a marginal cost curve. This means that producers are only going to be willing to produce and sell one more unit of a good if they're guaranteed to receive a price for selling it that is greater than the cost of making it.

This, then, brings us to the concept of allocative efficiency. In this market for pizza, if the price of pizza is $10, then 50 pizzas will be demanded, 50 pizzas will be supplied, and we will be in equilibrium. But remember, the demand curve is also the marginal benefit curve, which is telling us that when 50 pizzas are consumed, the marginal benefit, the additional benefit, of consuming that 50th pizza (just that last pizza) is $10. The last pizza that was sold brought an additional benefit of $10 to the person who consumed it. And when we say that supply is marginal cost, what we mean is that when that 50th pizza is produced, the additional cost to businesses of producing that last pizza is $10. So in this market, in equilibrium, when we produce 50 pizzas, the marginal benefit of consuming that 50th pizza is $10, and the marginal cost of producing that 50th pizza is also $10. Marginal benefit equals marginal cost: the additional benefit to consumers of consuming that 50th pizza is equal to the additional cost to producers of making that 50th pizza. This is what we call allocative efficiency. Allocative efficiency is achieved in the market when marginal benefit equals marginal cost.
