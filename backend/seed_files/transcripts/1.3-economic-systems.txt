So far, we've identified the four factors of production that an economy has available to it: land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship. We also understand the idea that these factors of production are scarce, due to the fact that they're available in finite quantities, but human wants and needs, or uses for those factors of production, are boundless, or seemingly unlimited. This scarcity then requires us to make decisions about how these factors of production get put to use. The mechanism by which factors of production are allocated is determined in large part by the economic system in place, which is the topic of today's video. Hi, I'm Mr. Racine. Welcome to today's episode of Economics Videos That Don't Suck.

Consider a farmer who's trying to decide between the production of corn or the production of wheat. At the beginning of the year, he has a certain amount of land, labor, and capital available to him. How does he decide how much of his resources to allocate to the production of corn, and how much to allocate to the production of wheat? And once those goods and services are produced, how is it determined who gets to consume them? These are the fundamental questions that all economies must answer. One: what gets produced? Two: how does it get produced? And three: who consumes it? Or, once it's produced, how is it allocated? The way that these questions are resolved is determined by the economic system in place.

So it's possible our farmer here lives in a monarchy, where the king makes all of the economic decisions. In fact, the king owns everything: the land, the capital, it all belongs to the state. At the beginning of the year, the king tells the farmer whether to grow corn or wheat or both. The farmer's only concern is meeting the quota, or the required quantity of the particular crop set out by the king. This would be an example of what we call a command economic system. In the command economic system, all factors of production are owned and controlled by a central authority figure, who makes all economic decisions of what to produce, how to produce it, and how to allocate it.

After the Chinese Communist Revolution in 1949, the government seized most of the land and capital, proclaiming it property of the state and implementing a centrally planned command economic system. The government set out quotas for farming communes to meet, dictating the crops that they grew. Output was rationed; you couldn't technically buy rice, you needed your weekly ration ticket. The government went so far as to control where people could live and what jobs they could hold.

So in our farmer example, if the king is smart and good and kind and benevolent and forward-thinking, he'll command the farmer to produce the socially optimal quantity of wheat and corn. Let's say half of the people in the kingdom like to eat bread, which requires wheat, and half of the people in the kingdom like to eat cornbread, which requires corn. The king commands the farmer to grow half corn, half wheat, and everyone is happy. Output is what we call, in economics, allocatively efficient.

Okay, but what if now, over time, tastes change, and more and more people start to eat cornbread? But the king is stuck in his traditional ways, and he continues to command the farmer to grow half wheat and half corn. As the demand grows for corn, there starts to be a surplus in the production of wheat and a shortage in the production of corn. The farmer's only concerned with keeping his head attached to his shoulders by keeping the king happy, so he says nothing and grows what the king has told him to grow, and the shortages and surpluses grow, representing now an allocatively inefficient level of output. So if the central planner makes the right decisions, then command economic systems theoretically have the ability to provide for all in an equitable and plentiful fashion. But as our farmer example shows, command economic systems are vulnerable to poor economic decision making by central planners.

So in the case of our farmer, what might be a good alternative to the command economic system? Well, it seems obvious that the farmer can arrive at a more allocatively efficient level of output by paying attention to the shortages and surpluses that were created by the market, rather than by sticking to the whims of a foolish king. An economic system where there is no central planner, but economic decisions are instead made by the interactions of buyers and sellers, is what we refer to as a market economic system. In a market economic system, individuals own all of the factors of production. Firms make output decisions based upon the goal of maximizing their profits, and consumers influence market outcomes by buying the products that maximize their own benefit. Prices are determined by the influences of supply and demand, and output is allocated according to the pricing mechanism.

So in our example, once people start to want to eat more cornbread and less bread, we would expect the increased demand for corn to drive up the price of corn, thereby giving the farmer incentive to produce more corn and less wheat. If somewhere down the line people decide they want to eat more bread, thereby increasing the demand for wheat, the same mechanism will incentivize the farmer to shift more resources into the production of wheat. So this market economic system seems great in a lot of circumstances. It enables suppliers to be responsive to market changes, and uses the pricing mechanism to send signals to producers to increase or decrease output.

In a pure market economy, there is no government. All goods and services are provided through the private market, with output and pricing levels determined by supply and demand. But the farmer isn't the only one in our economy. Let's also consider the chief of police. If we're in a pure market economy, the police will offer their protection, but only for a fee, so everybody would have to subscribe to the police service. But if this chief of police were, shall we say, unscrupulous, he might realize that it is more profitable to charge a really high fee, so that he only has to provide law enforcement to a select portion of the population. Or even worse, he could charge a fee for protection in the same way that mobsters do in Scorsese films or The Sopranos. You can see where this is going. Clearly, some industries don't work in a pure market economy. One could envision similar problems if we only had private businesses building and providing access to roads, airports, schools, et cetera.

For this reason, there are no pure market economies. All economies are, in fact, mixed economies. In mixed economies, some economic activities are dictated by the free market, while others are directed by a central authority, most often the government. So the U.S. is a mixed economy. The price of Xboxes and the total quantity of haircuts performed in the U.S., for the most part, are determined by the buyers and sellers of those products, with minimal government intervention. However, the government does provide free K-12 education to all, and sets the minimum wage that employers are allowed to pay their employees: instances of decisions being made by a central authority.

All economies exist somewhere on the spectrum of mixed economies, with some closer to command and some closer to market. North Korea would fall closer to a command economy, with significant central planning for output distribution, and even control over labor markets and the jobs individuals can hold. But even there, at the height of economic control, in the most isolated of economies and with the most authoritarian government, some market activities still took place through informal markets, where citizens secured food and other necessities that the rationing systems failed to provide. Switzerland would represent an economy that falls closer on the spectrum to a market economy. It engages in free and open trade with other economies, labor regulations are relatively flexible, and it's relatively easy to open and operate a business.

One final point: in order for market economies to function smoothly, there need to be strong individual property rights laws, which are what enable individuals to own the factors of production and use them to create output and generate profits. So, ironically, a pure market economy can't exist without the very thing that would make it not a pure market economy: a government.

So there you have it: three different economic systems. Command economic system: a central authority makes all decisions regarding output, all factors of production are publicly owned, and output is often allocated via rationing methods. Market economic system: the forces of supply and demand determine the makeup of output and prices, all factors of production are privately owned, and the pricing mechanism determines the allocation of output. And mixed economic systems: a combination of command and market economic systems. All economies are mixed economies, with some falling further down the spectrum towards market-oriented economies and others closer to command-type economies.
